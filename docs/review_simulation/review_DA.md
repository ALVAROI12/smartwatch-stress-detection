### Strongest Counter-Argument

The paper labels stressor stages "stress" and resting stages "non-stress", trains a gradient-boosted model on heart-rate and electrodermal features, and then finds that the model responds to heart rate and electrodermal level. That result is built into the design. It says nothing specific about wrist devices, and it does not show that evaluative stress lacks a physiological signature. The decisive "matched-arousal test" conditions on an index made from the model's two strongest features. Once those are held roughly constant, little variance is left to rank, so AUROC falls towards 0.5 for any pair. The paper's own reference row shows this: stress against baseline/rest, the contrast the model was trained on, drops to 0.584. The UBFC-Phys evaluation contrast gives 0.577, essentially the same number. The first is called "significant" (n = 5,445 windows) and the second "chance" (n = 190, 8 control subjects). The difference between the two verdicts is sample size, not mechanism.

The one variant that is truly non-circular (heart-rate index, EDA-only model) makes Lego separable (0.73–0.84). The paper sets it aside as "not comparable". The probes are also not verified non-stress states: the Lego task has a countdown, the dataset's own authors tag hyperventilation as stress-inducing, and seated arithmetic without a panel counts as "stress" in PhysioNet and Campanella but as "control" in UBFC-Phys. A detector that fires on these states may simply be right.

"Stressor potency" is never measured independently of the physiology the detector uses. The one independent measure, self-rated stress, does not predict misses, and on WESAD it runs the wrong way (ρ = −0.60). The simpler reading: stage-label detectors track sympathetic change, and the paper's instruments cannot show whether anything beyond that exists.

### Ignored Alternative Explanations/Paths
1. **The probes are partial stressors.** Lego under countdown, hyperventilation, and unevaluated arithmetic all involve load or threat. Detection of them is then correct, and "false-stress" is mislabelled. This fits the specificity panel as well as the arousal thesis does, and it explains within-bin AUROCs below 0.5 (the probe is scored as more stress-like than the stressor).
2. **Circular conditioning, not the absence of a signature.** The matched-arousal AUROC falls for *every* pair, including the trained one (0.584). The parsimonious reading is that the model's score is roughly a function of HR and EDA. That is a statement about the features, not about stress.
3. **Protocol-shape transfer.** All five datasets put a seated rest first and the stressor later, on the same device. Transfer may reflect shared structure (warm-up, drift, stage order) that per-subject z-scoring turns into a common signal. Position-matched negatives bound this at 0.03–0.04 but draw mostly on post-stressor rest.
4. **Ceiling from label timing and non-response, not potency.** PhysioNet's second-half-rest sensitivity (0.746 to 0.797, reported in the project notes) and the strict responder filter (+0.09) show that label construction moves the ceiling at least as much as any potency measure does.
5. **Instrument ceiling.** Wrist EDA agrees with palmar EDA at r ≈ 0.3. An evaluative signature, if one exists, may be invisible to this sensor. That is a device limit, not a statement about what "detectors detect".

### Missing Stakeholder Perspectives
- Psychophysiologists and stress-appraisal researchers: whether "evaluative threat" should have a peripheral autonomic signature at all.
- Clinical and occupational end users (for example, nurses and shift workers), whose false alarms come from daily-life arousal rather than lab probes.
- Dataset curators, on the stress status of their control and probe conditions.
- Device manufacturers, since the thesis is framed at the level of the device.

### Unexamined Premise
Throughout, the paper assumes that "stress" is a state separate from "arousal" and should be detectable as such. If acute stress is defined as appraisal-dependent sympathetic activation, then "detects arousal, not stress" is not a finding. It is a claim about what the labels can support, and the reporting standards should target label design rather than detectors.

### Observations (Non-Defects)
- The label audit, subject-grouped evaluation, corrected tests and the UBFC-Phys missing-temperature diagnosis are strong and would survive every attack above.
- The clean variants in `partA_disjoint.csv` give the authors a *better* argument than the one they make. There, the baseline/rest reference rises to 0.63–0.68 while UBFC control is 0.47–0.49. Recast as a probe-against-reference contrast with CIs, this could partly answer C1 for UBFC, though still with n = 8.
- The time-only classifier result (0.95 on Stress-Predict) is a useful, independent warning for the field, whether or not the arousal thesis holds.
- The paper states its limitations candidly (transductive scaling, small cells, EDA fidelity). The problem is that the title and conclusion do not carry those limitations through.
