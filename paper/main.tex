\title{What Do Wrist-Worn Stress Detectors Detect? Arousal Transfers Across Five Empatica E4 Datasets, Evaluative Stress Does Not}

\author{Alvaro~Octavio~Ibarra~Reyes and Chen~Pan%
\thanks{The authors are with the Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, The University of Texas at San Antonio, San Antonio, TX 78249 USA (e-mail: [email]@utsa.edu; [email]@utsa.edu).}}

\markboth{IEEE Journal of Biomedical and Health Informatics}{Ibarra Reyes and Pan: What Do Wrist-Worn Stress Detectors Detect?}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Wrist-worn stress detectors are trained to separate stressor periods from rest and are reported as detecting stress. We ask what they detect. On five public Empatica E4 datasets with audited protocol-stage labels, leave-one-dataset-out evaluation on held-out subjects, per-subject normalisation and corrected significance tests, a model of heart-rate, heart-rate-variability and electrodermal features trained on four datasets loses only 0.015--0.05 balanced accuracy on the fifth; domain adaptation, few-shot personalisation and per-user thresholds add nothing significant. We score one fixed detector on non-stress arousal states it was never shown. Adding a state to the training negatives removes exercise false alarms (69\% to 8\%) but not false alarms on speech and arithmetic without evaluation (55\% to 56\%), hyperventilation (about 50\%), manual tasks (43\% to 33\%) or film-induced emotions. At equal autonomic arousal the detector cannot separate stress from the same tasks without evaluation, hyperventilation, manual tasks or anger clips (AUROC 0.36--0.58, none different from chance after Holm correction), but separates it from exercise (0.89). Protocol position alone predicts stress better than physiology on the two hardest datasets, whose baselines are recorded while the sensor is still warming up. A test-side error budget attributes at most 0.06 of the 0.22--0.29 within-dataset error to recovery, sensor loss and onset latency; a strict responder filter recovers up to 0.09 on one dataset. Wrist detectors transfer because they detect autonomic arousal; the ceiling is stressor potency. We propose a specificity panel and a matched-arousal test as reporting standards and release audited labels, frozen subject splits and code.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Wearable stress detection, Empatica E4, autonomic arousal, cross-dataset generalization, specificity, electrodermal activity, heart rate variability.

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{S}{tress} detection from wrist wearables is usually posed as a classification problem: windows recorded during a laboratory stressor are labelled stress, windows recorded during rest or a control task are labelled non-stress, and a model is trained to separate them. Reported accuracies on the standard datasets are high, and the models are described as detecting stress. Two observations motivate this paper. First, the cross-dataset literature disagrees about whether such models transfer: Prajod et al.~\cite{prajod2024} and Calza-Metre and Borz\`i~\cite{calzametre2026} report large drops when the stressor changes, Xiao et al.~\cite{xiao2025} report balanced accuracy falling from 0.88 to 0.53--0.69 across seven wrist datasets, whereas Mishra et al.~\cite{mishra2020} found a cost of about 0.01 between two Empatica E4 studies with the same stressor, and Kwon et al.~\cite{kwon2026} found near-perfect transfer to WESAD but near-chance results on Stress-Predict. Second, the wrist device measures pulse rate, a weak vagal proxy, wrist electrodermal activity (EDA), skin temperature and movement. None of these is stress; all of them respond to autonomic arousal from any cause. Kwon et al.~\cite{kwon2026} matched heart rate within subject and found that WESAD stress remains separable from baseline through EDA and temperature, which rules out a heart-rate shortcut there. Whether a detector separates stress from other arousing non-stress states when heart rate and EDA are matched together has not, to our knowledge, been tested.

We address both observations with the same five public E4 datasets. After auditing every dataset's labels against its descriptor paper, validating wrist cardiac features against chest electrocardiography (ECG), and fixing an evaluation protocol with subject-grouped splits and corrected tests, we show that the transfer cost is small once labels and per-subject normalisation are right, and that the remaining disagreement in the literature is largely about absolute difficulty rather than transfer. We then ask what transfers. A specificity panel scores one fixed detector on arousal states absent from training, and a matched-arousal test asks whether stress and non-stress arousal remain separable at equal arousal. Both answer that the detector separates exercise from stress, and fear clips once it has seen them, but not evaluative stress from the same tasks performed without evaluation, from hyperventilation, from manual tasks, or from anger clips. A time-only classifier, a warm-up analysis and a test-side error budget then locate the remaining within-dataset ceiling in the stressor rather than in the pipeline.

The contributions are: (1) a five-dataset leave-one-dataset-out benchmark with audited protocol-stage labels, showing a transfer cost of 0.015--0.05 balanced accuracy and diagnosing one apparent domain gap as a missing channel; (2) a specificity panel and a matched-arousal test showing that wrist detectors separate exercise but not non-evaluative arousal from stress; (3) a quantification of time in session and sensor warm-up as confounds, with a position-matched evaluation rule; (4) an error budget showing that recovery contamination, sensor loss and onset latency explain at most 0.06 of the within-dataset error, and that a strict responder filter recovers at most a further 0.09 on one dataset; (5) leakage-controlled negative results for domain adaptation, few-shot personalisation, per-user thresholds, self-rated stress intensity and a subject-level detectability trait. Labels, frozen splits and scripts are released. Section~II reviews related work, Section~III describes the datasets and the label audit, Section~IV the features, normalisation and evaluation rules, Section~V the results, and Section~VI discusses implications, reporting norms and limitations.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Cross-dataset transfer}
Kwon et al.~\cite{kwon2026} ran pooled leave-one-dataset-out (LODO) evaluation on three E4 corpora with logistic regression and gradient boosting and no per-subject normalisation, obtaining an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of about 0.90 with WESAD as target but 0.52--0.57 on Stress-Predict and the Nurse dataset; their within-dataset AUROC on Stress-Predict was already 0.63, so the transfer cost there is 0.06--0.08. Calza-Metre and Borz\`i~\cite{calzametre2026} report pairwise zero-shot transfer across four E4 corpora, with WESAD macro-F1 falling from 0.87 within to 0.63 from Campanella. Xiao et al.~\cite{xiao2025} train on one or two of seven wrist datasets and observe balanced accuracy 0.53--0.69 out of distribution. Mishra et al.~\cite{mishra2020}, with per-participant normalisation and matched arithmetic stressors, found a median AUROC cost of about 0.01. Schreiber and Maleshkova~\cite{schreiber2026} pool three datasets under leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) evaluation and name LODO as future work. Prajod et al.~\cite{prajod2024} attribute transfer failure to stressor type, on chest ECG. Our reading of this literature is that the reported failures come from low within-dataset ceilings, from stressor mismatch, or from the absence of per-subject normalisation, and the present study tests each.

\subsection{Label quality and confounds}
Several authors name label noise as a cause of poor transfer~\cite{kwon2026,singh2026feel} but none audits and fixes labels and measures the effect. Mishra et al.~\cite{mishra2020} exclude post-stressor rest because it carries residual arousal; Aydo\u{g}an and Villagra Povina~\cite{aydogan2026} found independently that the PhysioNet stress sessions mix rest and stressor blocks. Richer et al.~\cite{richer2024} note that comparing a stressor to a pre-stress baseline confounds the comparison with sequence effects. Tognotti et al.~\cite{tognotti2026} show that test-inclusive baseline normalisation inflates balanced accuracy by 3--13 points, and Fecke and Rehof~\cite{fecke2026} normalise from a calibration baseline on chest ECG. Aydo\u{g}an and Villagra Povina~\cite{aydogan2026} report that an XGBoost model labels 82.6\% of PhysioNet exercise windows as stress, and 6--7\% once exercise is a training class. Saeb et al.~\cite{saeb2017} and Bahameish et al.~\cite{bahameish2024} document the inflation from record-wise cross-validation. Schmidt et al.~\cite{schmidt2019} recommend leave-one-subject-out validation and treat physical activity as a confound for which it is ``sufficient to estimate the intensity level of an activity''. Vos et al.~\cite{vos2023}, found that apart from two studies, including Mishra et al.~\cite{mishra2020}, none of the reviewed work tested its models on a totally unseen dataset, and estimate that ``at least 34 test subjects would be required to achieve 80\% statistical power''.

\subsection{Personalisation and the arousal question}
Adding a few labelled windows of a new user to a generic model is well established~\cite{stewart2020,han2024,tervonen2026,akkaya2026}. Where calibration windows are taken chronologically, gains are small: Tervonen et al.~\cite{tervonen2026} found retraining XGBoost gained 1.7 balanced-accuracy points on WESAD with 1--3 windows per class, and Akkaya~\cite{akkaya2026} reports that the few-shot benefit did not survive a prospective chronological protocol. On wrist cardiac validity, Milstein and Gordon~\cite{milstein2020} found that the root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD) from the E4 correlated with ECG at $r=0.42$ during speech, and Watanabe et al.~\cite{watanabe2025} found the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST) to be the worst condition for E4 beat detection on WESAD. Sosa et al.~\cite{sosa2026}, reviewing multimodal arousal classifiers, state that ``physiological signals primarily index arousal and regulatory processes rather than affective valence'' and that sympathetic activation ``can therefore arise not only during stress, but also during non-stressful states''; the reviewed studies did not test classifiers on such states. Kwon et al.~\cite{kwon2026} balanced heart rate within 5-bpm bins per subject: on WESAD the all-feature AUROC moved from 0.955 to 0.938 and EDA plus temperature alone kept 0.935, while on Stress-Predict every matched feature set stayed weak (EDA plus temperature 0.607). Their contrast is stress against baseline; the matched-arousal test below instead matches heart rate and EDA jointly and contrasts stress with other arousing states.

The gap is therefore twofold. No study has scored one fixed, subject-independent wrist detector across a panel of non-stress arousal states, or asked whether stress remains separable from those states at equal arousal; and no study has decomposed the within-dataset ceiling of wrist stress detection into recovery, sensor, onset, responder and stressor terms. The present paper addresses both on five public datasets with audited labels.

\section{Datasets and Label Audit}
Five public datasets recorded with the Empatica E4 (blood volume pulse (BVP) 64\,Hz, EDA 4\,Hz, skin temperature 4\,Hz, accelerometer 32\,Hz) contain both stress and non-stress periods: WESAD~\cite{schmidt2018wesad} (15 subjects; TSST speech and arithmetic before a panel, standing), the PhysioNet stress and exercise dataset of Hongn et al.~\cite{hongn2025data} (35; Stroop and the Trier Mental Challenge Test (TMCT) of mental arithmetic under time pressure, seated, plus aerobic and anaerobic sessions; its opinion tasks yield no complete window), Stress-Predict~\cite{iqbal2022} (34 after exclusion; Stroop and a TSST-style interview, plus a hyperventilation block that its descriptor lists among the stress-inducing tasks), UBFC-Phys~\cite{meziati2023} (19; speech and arithmetic in a harder ``test'' version, a job interview and a countdown in steps of 17, or an easier ``control'' version, a holiday speech and a countdown in steps of 10, both spoken aloud to the experimenter; the authors note that both versions may ``imply a high stress level'') and Campanella~2024~\cite{campanella2024} (29; backward subtraction, plus three Lego assembly tasks designed to create work-like ``mental strain'', one with a spoken countdown; the closing CV presentation lies in the untagged tail and is excluded). EPM-E4~\cite{garcia2020epm} (33 subjects with clip timing) supplies film-induced anger, fear, happiness and sadness and is used only as a probe. Table~\ref{tab:harm} gives the harmonised classes.

Every labelling decision was checked against the dataset's descriptor paper. Seven corrections followed: in WESAD, labels had to be taken from the synchronised file rather than mapped by length onto the raw E4 export, whose recording starts 16--26 min earlier; in PhysioNet, only the tagged stressor tasks count as stress, not the whole stress session, one subject with a documented bad fit (f13) is excluded, and the 3 min before the first tag serve as baseline; in Stress-Predict, subject S01 violated the protocol and is dropped, and task boundaries whose tags are more than 180 s from the logged time are dropped; in Campanella, which logs no per-subject task markers, the subtraction span ends at a per-subject end estimated from the recording length. In addition, the Stress-Predict descriptor tags its 2-min hyperventilation block as a ``stress-inducing task''; it is a respiratory manoeuvre with no evaluative component, and we keep it as a separate probe class. The three participants recorded in two files are treated as one subject each, so that no person appears on both sides of a split.

A cautionary note on evaluation. An earlier version of this pipeline used window-level train/test splitting with 50\%-overlapping windows and reported 94\% accuracy on a six-class task (92\% in our reproduction); the same model scores 49\% (balanced accuracy 0.36) when 15\% of subjects are held out (\texttt{leakage\_check.csv}). Record-wise splitting~\cite{saeb2017} remains common in this field, and every number below uses subject-grouped splits.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Features}
Windows are 60 s at a 30-s step, anchored inside one protocol stage. Cardiac features, the mean and standard deviation (SD) of heart rate (HR) and three heart-rate-variability (HRV) measures (RMSSD, the SD of normal-to-normal intervals (SDNN) and the proportion of successive differences above 50 ms (pNN50)), come from BVP beats detected with NeuroKit2 after band-pass filtering and artefact rejection; windows with fewer than 50\% usable beats carry missing cardiac values. Against WESAD chest ECG on the same 1,398 windows, wrist heart rate has a mean absolute error of 2.4 bpm ($r=0.94$); RMSSD is weak ($r=0.53$, bias +34 ms), and only 37\% of TSST windows keep usable photoplethysmography (PPG) because speech moves the wrist (heart-rate bias \m7 bpm there). EDA features are mean, SD, range, slope, tonic and phasic means, and skin conductance response (SCR) count and amplitude. Temperature features are included only in the ``physiology'' set; the main set is HR/HRV/EDA, for reasons given in Sections~V-B and V-E. Accelerometer features are used only in the modality comparison.

\subsection{Normalisation}
Features are z-scored per subject using all windows of that subject's stress-protocol recording, without labels. This is transductive; two non-transductive variants are reported as sensitivity analyses: raw features, and causal scaling from strictly earlier windows only (the first four windows share the statistics of that opening period).

\subsection{Task, models and evaluation}
The main task is stress versus all non-stress states (baseline, rest, meditation, amusement). Baseline-versus-stress alone is confounded with time in session (Section~V-E). The classifier is XGBoost with fixed hyperparameters; a nested-tuning comparison of XGBoost, random forest and a multilayer perceptron found one significant difference in 72 comparisons after Holm correction, on the nine-class affective task only and none on the stress tasks (\texttt{significance\_models.csv}), so tuning is not repeated. Evaluation uses 20 subject-grouped splits with 20\% of each dataset's subjects held out, LOSO where per-subject scores are needed, and LODO in which the target's held-out subjects are scored by a model trained (a) on the target's other subjects, (b) on the other four datasets only, or (c) on both. Paired comparisons use Nadeau--Bengio corrected $t$-tests~\cite{nadeau2003} with Holm correction; per-subject rates carry subject-bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals (CI). The threshold is 0.5 throughout; balanced accuracy (BA) and AUROC are reported.

\subsection{Four evaluation rules}
\emph{Specificity panel.} A fixed detector is scored on non-stress states absent from its task definition, reporting the false-stress rate ($p>0.5$) under three training conditions: the state's dataset absent from training; all datasets in training but the state itself absent; the state added to the training negatives. \emph{Matched-arousal test.} A label-free arousal index (mean of per-subject z-scored heart rate and tonic EDA, or tonic EDA alone where heart rate is missing) is computed per window; stress windows are pooled with one non-stress arousal state, binned into arousal quintiles, and the AUROC of the stress score is computed within bins and averaged by bin size. An AUROC near 0.5 means the model has learned nothing beyond arousal magnitude for that pair. \emph{Position-matched negatives.} Non-stress test windows are restricted to the 5th--95th percentile range of minutes-since-start spanned by that dataset's stress windows. \emph{Test-side error budget.} Filters applied to out-of-sample predictions without retraining, with gains Shapley-averaged over all filter orders.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Subject-independent performance}
On the pooled baseline-versus-stress task of WESAD and PhysioNet (50 subjects) with raw (unnormalised) features and the fixed-hyperparameter model, 20 subject-grouped splits give BA 0.83 (Nadeau--Bengio 95\% CI 0.79--0.88) with all modalities, 0.81 (0.76--0.86) with physiology only and 0.75 (0.68--0.82) with the accelerometer only; LOSO agrees (0.85, 0.81, 0.75). No modality difference survives Holm correction ($p_{\mathrm{holm}}=1.0$, \texttt{significance\_modalities.csv}), and a complete 108-configuration nested tuning run changes none of these conclusions. The model is therefore not detecting movement alone, and physiology carries most of the signal. On the main task and all five datasets, within-dataset BA with HR/HRV/EDA features is 0.916 (WESAD), 0.882 (Campanella), 0.877 (UBFC-Phys), 0.776 (PhysioNet) and 0.697 (Stress-Predict) (Table~\ref{tab:lodo}). The two lowest ceilings belong to the two datasets whose stressors are seated, mostly silent and weakly evaluative, and whose electrodermal effect sizes are smallest: mean EDA rises by 0.4 z in PhysioNet and 0.7 z in Stress-Predict against 1.7--2.2 z in the other three (\texttt{stress\_effect\_size\_all\_datasets.csv}).

With HR/HRV/EDA features, a model trained on the other four datasets loses 0.03 (WESAD), 0.03 (PhysioNet), 0.05 (Stress-Predict), 0.04 (UBFC-Phys) and 0.015 (Campanella) BA against the within-dataset model on the same held-out subjects. None of the five costs is significant after Holm correction (Nadeau--Bengio $p_{\mathrm{holm}}\ge 0.20$; only Stress-Predict excludes zero before correction, 0.052 [0.003, 0.101]), but with 3--7 held-out subjects per split the upper 95\% bounds reach 0.08--0.13 (\texttt{D\_lodo\_cost\_corrected.csv}). We therefore claim no large cost, not equality.

\emph{An untrained arousal index transfers as well as the model.} If the detector has learned arousal magnitude, a label-free arousal index should do nearly as well. The index (mean of per-subject z-scored heart rate and tonic EDA, EDA alone where heart rate is missing) needs no training; only its threshold is set, on the other four datasets. Table~\ref{tab:lodo} gives its external BA: 0.836 on WESAD, 0.743 on PhysioNet, 0.657 on Stress-Predict, 0.762 on UBFC-Phys and 0.877 on Campanella, with AUROC 0.73--0.95. The trained model beats it by 0.048 on WESAD and 0.075 on UBFC-Phys, ties on PhysioNet (+0.003), and trails it on Stress-Predict and Campanella; no difference survives Holm correction (paired Nadeau--Bengio, $p_{\mathrm{holm}}\ge 0.14$; \texttt{C\_arousal\_index\_lodo.csv}). An EDA-only index loses significantly to the model on PhysioNet ($-0.15$) and a heart-rate-only index on UBFC-Phys and Campanella, so neither channel alone suffices. Up to the power of these tests, a model trained on four datasets of stressor labels behaves on the fifth like an untrained two-channel arousal index.

\emph{A missing channel posing as a domain gap.} With temperature included, external UBFC-Phys falls to 0.589 BA while its AUROC stays 0.931 and an oracle threshold recovers 0.910 (\texttt{threshold\_transfer\_probe\_summary.csv}). UBFC-Phys has no temperature in any window, so XGBoost's missing-value branch pushes its mean stress score to 0.18 against 0.31--0.42 on the other targets. Ranking is intact; the threshold is not. Dropping temperature lifts external UBFC-Phys to 0.837. Elsewhere the remaining external loss is mostly ranking: an oracle threshold recovers only 0.02--0.05 with HR/HRV/EDA features, and a label-free cut matching the source stress rate hurts Campanella (0.867 to 0.732).

\emph{Normalisation.} The small cost does not depend on transductive scaling. With raw features the external cost is \m0.01 to 0.05 BA; causal scaling keeps it on WESAD (0.845 within, 0.841 external), PhysioNet (0.774, 0.744) and Stress-Predict (0.688, 0.665) but loses about 0.10 externally on UBFC-Phys and Campanella, whose recordings are short (7--11 windows per subject). Whole-session scaling mainly raises the absolute level on both sides. No external difference between variants survives Holm correction across 60 tests.

\emph{Label fixes.} Every external score rose after the seven corrections (for example UBFC-Phys 0.790 to 0.837, whose own data did not change), but on the two datasets where a paired test is possible only 1 of 16 differences survives Holm correction (WESAD external AUROC, +0.0075, $p_{\mathrm{holm}}=0.021$). In the two-dataset setting of Kwon et al.~\cite{kwon2026} (WESAD and Stress-Predict), the fixes change transfer by at most 0.02; our pipeline nevertheless reaches external AUROC 0.67 on Stress-Predict against their 0.56, and per-subject scaling raises WESAD as target from 0.834 to 0.914 AUROC. The label audit is a correctness contribution; the small transfer cost comes from features, task definition and normalisation.

The LODO task keeps only baseline, rest, meditation and amusement as negatives; other recorded states were never scored. Table~\ref{tab:panel} and Fig.~\ref{fig:panel}(a) give their false-stress rates. Exercise is the one probed state that is unambiguously not stress. A model that never saw exercise calls 69--70\% of PhysioNet exercise windows stress, matching the 71--83\% of Aydo\u{g}an and Villagra Povina~\cite{aydogan2026}; adding exercise to the training negatives collapses this to 8--10\%, and the external PhysioNet drop is significant (BA 0.746 to 0.583, $p_{\mathrm{holm}}=0.011$). No other state behaves this way, but the other probes are not clean non-stress states. The UBFC-Phys control version is an easier social task that its authors expect to be stressful, the Campanella Lego tasks were built to induce work-like strain, the Stress-Predict descriptor lists hyperventilation among its stress-inducing tasks, and the EPM-E4 clips are passive viewing. The UBFC-Phys control version is called stress at 0.55--0.57 whether or not the model has seen it; hyperventilation rises from 0.42 to 0.54--0.55 once Stress-Predict is in training; Lego tasks fall only from 0.43 to 0.33; and the EPM-E4 clips fall by 0.05--0.10. Adding a probed state to the negatives changes stress recall by at most $-$0.05 (UBFC-Phys 0.74 to 0.70 with Lego added; PhysioNet 0.74 to 0.71 and Campanella 0.92 to 0.89 with the EPM-E4 clips added), and exercise negatives raise it (Stress-Predict 0.63 to 0.70, UBFC-Phys 0.74 to 0.79), so the failure is not a sensitivity trade-off. The hyperventilation (1.7 windows per subject), anger (one window per subject) and UBFC-Phys control (8 subjects) cells are small; Lego and the fear clips carry the statistics.

The reference row sets the scale: at matched arousal, even the trained contrast, stress against baseline and rest, reaches only 0.584 for the external model. The wrist features therefore carry little stress information beyond arousal magnitude, whatever the comparator. \subsection{Time in session and sensor warm-up}
\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_time.pdf}

(b), (c) Subject-centred skin temperature and tonic EDA against minutes since the first protocol window (2-min bins); the shaded band marks the first 10 min, in which 100\% of Campanella, 97\% of Stress-Predict and 68\% of PhysioNet baseline windows fall when measured from recording start (90\% within 15 min). \label{fig:time}
\end{figure*}

Baseline is recorded first in every dataset, so any baseline-referenced comparison is confounded with time in session. Fig.~\ref{fig:time}(a) quantifies this: minutes since recording start, used as the only feature, reaches BA 0.83 on PhysioNet (physiology 0.78; better in 16 of 20 splits) and 0.95 on Stress-Predict (physiology 0.70; 20 of 20), ties on WESAD (0.90 against 0.92) and is perfect on Campanella. Given minutes as an extra feature, the within-dataset model exploits it (Stress-Predict 0.70 to 0.95) and then collapses externally (0.66 to 0.56; WESAD 0.90 to 0.45), because protocol timing does not transfer. Any within-dataset pipeline that leaks time, through raw temperature, drift features or the absence of per-subject normalisation, inherits this. The physiology model survives position-matched negatives with changes of at most \m0.03 within and \m0.04 externally (\texttt{time\_probe\_summary.csv}), so the LODO result is not riding on time; the matched negatives are mostly post-stressor rest, so this also bounds recovery contamination. A baseline-referenced feature set, by contrast, calls 50--83\% of later non-stress windows stress across the WESAD--PhysioNet pair (\texttt{order\_confound\_check.csv}).

Time is informative on these datasets because baseline coincides with the warm-up phase of the sensor. Measured from the start of each E4 recording (\texttt{warmup\_since\_donning.csv}), subject-centred skin temperature rises at 0.02--0.08 $^\circ$C/min in the first 10 min on all four datasets with a continuous clock keeps rising past 20 min on WESAD and rises until about minute 20 on Campanella before levelling off (+1.8 and +0.9 $^\circ$C between minutes 1--5 and 30--40; 13 of 15 WESAD subjects warm over the first 30 min, by up to +6.5 $^\circ$C), and tonic EDA rises in the first 10 min on all four. Baseline falls inside that phase on Campanella (100\% of baseline windows within 10 min of recording start), Stress-Predict (97\%) and PhysioNet (68\% within 10 min, 90\% within 15), and stress arrives at a median of 7--28 min. WESAD is the exception: its synchronised protocol starts 16--26 min after the raw E4 recording (median 21 min, found by matching the wrist EDA stream), so its baseline begins 17--49 min after the device was switched on (median 33 min) and no baseline window falls within 15 min of recording start. WESAD is also the one dataset on which the time-only classifier does not beat physiology. Whether recording start equals donning is not documented by any dataset; on WESAD, every subject's first minute of temperature reads 4--6 $^\circ$C above skin, which shows the device was placed within that minute. This is the quantitative form of the sequence effect described by Richer et al.~\cite{richer2024} and the reason temperature features track time and are excluded from the main feature set.

The responder filter is the one term sensitive to its definition (\texttt{partB\_responder\_sensitivity.csv}). At 0.5 or 1.0 baseline SD on either channel the two rules select the same subjects (32 of 35 PhysioNet, 32 of 34 Stress-Predict) and F4 gains 0.01--0.02. Requiring 1.0 SD on both channels keeps 17 of 35 PhysioNet subjects and raises within-dataset BA by 0.094 [0.05, 0.15] (external 0.065 [0.01, 0.13]); keeping the top half of subjects by pooled response gives 0.062 [0.01, 0.13]. On Stress-Predict the strict rules keep 12--17 of 34 subjects and gain 0.03 within (CI includes zero) and \m0.02 to +0.03 externally. Under the strict rules non-response is the largest single term on PhysioNet, about half of the four-filter total, yet the residual stays at 0.10--0.14 on PhysioNet and 0.21--0.35 on Stress-Predict. Onset latency and recovery are real but small: within-dataset Stress-Predict recall is 0.47 in the first minute of a stressor against 0.77 after 3 min, PhysioNet 0.63 in the first minute against 0.77 in the third, and rest false alarms peak in the first minute after offset (PhysioNet within 0.26 against 0.06--0.09 later); too few windows sit there to move BA.

On WESAD the only sizeable term is the sensor (F2, +0.045 external). Replacing wrist cardiac features with chest-ECG features on the same windows raises BA from 0.916 to 0.956 (AUROC 0.977 to 0.993), and chest ECG alone reaches 0.918, equal to the whole wrist pipeline; the gain concentrates in low-coverage windows, where wrist heart rate is absent (0.886 to 0.962). EDA alone gives 0.891. Motion-robust wrist heart rate is therefore worth at most about +0.04 on WESAD, and about 0.04 of error remains with chest-grade cardiac data.

Table~\ref{tab:neg} collects results that close directions the literature has proposed. Unsupervised domain adaptation on top of per-subject normalisation does not beat source-only training in either direction between WESAD and PhysioNet, consistent with Kwon et al.~\cite{kwon2026}. A per-user threshold hurts. \section{Discussion}
\emph{Arousal transfers, stress does not.} The results fit one reading. A wrist device sees sympathetic arousal through a low-fidelity window. Once labels are protocol-anchored and features are normalised per subject, the marginal distributions of that arousal are similar across laboratory datasets, so a detector transfers at small cost and domain adaptation has nothing to align. What the detector has learned is arousal magnitude, plus a cross-channel pattern that distinguishes exercise once exercise is in training; it has not learned evaluative threat, because at equal arousal it cannot tell an evaluated speech from an unevaluated one, a subtraction task from a Lego task, or a stressor from hyperventilation. Fear clips are the second separable state, and the disjoint variants place that residual in the cardiac features as much as in EDA, which is consistent with a distinct cardiac response to threat films rather than with an evaluative signature. The datasets with low ceilings are the ones whose stressors produce a sixth (PhysioNet) to a third (Stress-Predict) of the electrodermal response of the TSST in WESAD; filters for recovery, sensor loss and onset recover at most 0.06 of that gap, and a strict responder filter at most 0.09 on PhysioNet and nothing on Stress-Predict. The mild-stress, few-shot and domain-adaptation nulls follow: self-report is the wrong ruler for an arousal detector, and personalisation cannot recover a response that did not occur.

\emph{Reporting norms.} We propose that a wearable stress detector be published with a specificity panel: its false-stress rate under exercise, speech without evaluation, cognitive load without threat, and strong emotion, the way an assay reports cross-reactivity; and with a matched-arousal test where such states are available. We also propose three protocol rules: a settling period before baseline, or time since donning as a covariate; position-matched negatives, or at least a report of the time-only classifier, whenever baseline precedes the stressor; and exercise among the training negatives, or an activity gate, whenever the deployment includes movement.

\emph{What a decisive study needs.} No public E4 dataset separates speech from evaluation within subject or counterbalances order. The between-subject UBFC-Phys contrast is the closest, and it is small. A within-subject design with speech and evaluation crossed, cycling at matched heart rate, cognitive load without feedback, Latin-square order with baselines at both ends, chest ECG and a palmar EDA reference would settle whether wrist EDA carries an evaluative-threat signature that the present data cannot resolve, and would provide the first order-matched baseline on the wrist.

\emph{Limitations.} Whole-session per-subject normalisation is transductive; raw and causal variants keep the small transfer cost on long recordings but lose about 0.10 on short ones. The hyperventilation and UBFC-Phys control cells are small, and the UBFC-Phys contrast is between subjects. Wrist EDA agrees with palmar EDA at about $r=0.3$~\cite{milstein2020}, so a null on the evaluative-threat signature may be instrument failure rather than absence of the signature. The arousal index shares two features with the model; the feature-disjoint rerun holds, but under the heart-rate-only variant it cuts the usable Lego and hyperventilation pairs by 40--66\%. Responder filters use test labels and are diagnostic only; the strict definitions discard half the subjects. All data are laboratory recordings; ambulatory validity is untested. The pooled subject count (132 in the five stress datasets) is small for a field-level claim, and per-target costs of 0.015--0.05 are within the sampling error of 3--7 held-out subjects per split.

\section{Conclusion}
Across five public E4 datasets with audited labels, a wrist stress detector built on HR, HRV and EDA features transfers to an unseen dataset at a cost of 0.015--0.05 BA, and none of the methods tested here, from domain adaptation to few-shot calibration, improved on that. What transfers is autonomic arousal. The detector separates exercise from stress once shown exercise, and fear clips once shown them, but at equal arousal it cannot separate evaluative stress from the same tasks without evaluation, from hyperventilation, from manual work or from anger clips. Protocol position predicts stress better than physiology on the hardest datasets, whose baselines are recorded during sensor warm-up, and the within-dataset ceiling appears to be set by the stressor's potency and by subjects who do not respond, rather than by the pipeline. The next gains in wrist stress detection are more likely to come from protocol design, contextual sensing and honest abstention than from classifiers. Two studies follow from this work: a counterbalanced within-subject protocol that crosses speech with evaluation, adds matched-heart-rate exercise and non-evaluative cognitive load, and records chest ECG and palmar EDA alongside the wrist, to test whether wrist EDA carries an evaluative-threat signature at all; and an ambulatory validation in which the specificity panel is scored against labelled daily-life arousal, which no laboratory dataset can provide.
